Why Is Quantum Computing a Threat to Modern Cryptography?

Quantum computing is a major threat to modern cryptography because some mathematical problems that are extremely difficult for classical computers can be solved efficiently by sufficiently powerful quantum computers.

The most serious threat is to public-key cryptography, including RSA, Diffie–Hellman, and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC). Not all cryptographic algorithms are equally vulnerable.

1. Why Modern Cryptography Is Secure Today

Modern public-key cryptographic systems rely on mathematical problems that classical computers cannot solve efficiently with currently known algorithms.

Cryptographic algorithmMathematical security foundation
RSADifficulty of factoring large integers
Diffie–Hellman (DH)Difficulty of discrete logarithms
Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)Difficulty of elliptic curve discrete logarithms

Example: RSA

RSA generates two large prime numbers, p and q, and multiplies them:

N = p × q

For example, if p = 61 and q = 53, then:

N = 61 × 53 = 3233

Factoring 3233 is easy. But when RSA uses sufficiently large primes, factoring their product becomes extraordinarily difficult using known classical methods. For RSA-2048, the modulus N is 2048 bits long. An attacker who can factor N can derive the private key from the public key.

2. What Changes with Quantum Computing?

Quantum computers use quantum bits (qubits), superposition, interference, and entanglement. These enable computational approaches that differ fundamentally from classical algorithms.

  • Shor’s algorithm: threatens RSA, DH, and ECC.
  • Grover’s algorithm: reduces the idealized brute-force search complexity for symmetric keys.

3. Shor’s Algorithm: The Biggest Threat

A sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer could defeat the mathematical foundations of RSA, DH, and ECC.

In 1994, Peter Shor developed a quantum algorithm that solves integer factorization and discrete logarithms in polynomial time. These problems underlie major public-key systems.

Example: RSA-2048

Classical computerCryptographically capable quantum computer
No known efficient general factoring algorithmShor’s algorithm has polynomial-time complexity
Factoring RSA-2048 is infeasible with current classical technologyCould factor RSA-2048, given adequate fault-tolerant resources
RSA-2048 remains practical against known classical attacksRSA-2048 would become insecure

The crucial distinction is a change in computational complexity, not just faster execution of ordinary classical calculations.

Consequences

  • RSA: recover private keys from public keys.
  • Diffie–Hellman: recover secret exponents from public values.
  • ECDH: compromise elliptic curve key agreement.
  • ECDSA: recover signing keys and forge digital signatures.

This would impact TLS, VPNs, digital certificates, software signatures, and other security systems.

4. Grover’s Algorithm: Threat to Symmetric Encryption

Symmetric cryptography, including AES, is affected differently. Grover’s algorithm gives an idealized quadratic speedup for brute-force key search.

Classical: O(2k)   →   Quantum: O(2k/2)

Here, k is the key length in bits.

AlgorithmClassical brute-force complexityIdealized quantum search complexity
AES-1282128264
AES-1922192296
AES-25622562128
Important: Grover’s algorithm does not completely break AES. Practical quantum attacks have substantial hardware and error-correction costs, and AES-256 is generally considered appropriate for post-quantum symmetric encryption.

Bottom line: Quantum computing threatens widely used public-key algorithms much more severely than symmetric-key algorithms.

6. Why Is This a Threat to HTTPS and TLS?

Consider a simplified TLS connection using ECDHE to establish keys and AES to encrypt application data.

Conceptual illustration; actual TLS handshakes contain additional authentication and key-derivation messages.

  1. The client and server exchange public ECDHE values.
  2. They independently calculate a shared secret.
  3. They derive symmetric encryption keys.
  4. Application data is encrypted using AES.
  5. A future cryptographically capable quantum attacker could exploit recorded ECDHE public values to recover the shared secret and derive the session keys.

An attacker might record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it after obtaining a powerful quantum computer. This is called “Harvest Now, Decrypt Later” (HNDL).

7. Defending Against Quantum Attacks

The main approach is Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC): cryptographic schemes based on mathematical problems for which no efficient quantum attack is currently known. PQC runs on ordinary classical computers.

In August 2024, NIST finalized three important standards:

StandardAlgorithmPurpose
FIPS 203ML-KEM (derived from Kyber)Key encapsulation / establishment
FIPS 204ML-DSA (derived from Dilithium)Digital signatures
FIPS 205SLH-DSA (derived from SPHINCS+)Digital signatures

These mainly address vulnerable public-key operations, rather than replacing AES. A system can use post-quantum key establishment or hybrid key exchange, combining classical and post-quantum techniques.

8. Can Quantum Computers Break RSA Today?

As of November 2026, there is no publicly demonstrated quantum computer that can break properly implemented RSA-2048 or widely deployed ECC at cryptographically relevant sizes. Building large, reliable, fault-tolerant quantum computers remains challenging, and timelines are uncertain.

Because migration can take years, organizations should inventory vulnerable cryptography and plan transitions before such quantum attacks become practical.

9. Key Conceptual Takeaway

Known classical general-purpose integer-factoring methods are not polynomial time. Shor’s quantum factoring algorithm is polynomial time in the bit length n:

TShor(n) = poly(n)

That change in asymptotic complexity is fundamental. For symmetric encryption, Grover’s algorithm instead gives an idealized quadratic improvement over brute-force search, which can be countered largely by longer keys.

In one sentence: Quantum computing threatens modern public-key cryptography because Shor’s algorithm can efficiently solve the mathematical problems on which RSA, Diffie–Hellman, and ECC depend.
